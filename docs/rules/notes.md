# Notes and other commentators

The rules for the notes under the commentary: which commentators are brought and when, how a grammar note explains its point, how Ibn Ezra's ideas are explained, and what a note never says. Each rule gives the rule, an example and the reason; the numbers ("Ruling 43") are entries in **03 Rulings**. The last part, "For Claude", says how each rule is carried out and checked.

## The other commentators

### 1. Onkelos: a main commentator, on par with Rashi
**Rule.** A note brings Onkelos wherever his rendering bears on Ibn Ezra's reading — where he agrees with it, where he renders the word another way, and where Ibn Ezra cites "the Translator". It quotes the key Aramaic words, then translates them. The Targum's יְיָ prints as ה׳; a quotation never ends on a form of the Name with a prefix (דַּייָ, לַייָ) — the English says "of Hashem" instead. A note never says "with the Name"; it says "renders it as ה׳". The Onkelos line comes before Rashi, unless the Rashi line begins "Rashi, too" and refers back to Ibn Ezra; then Onkelos comes after it.

**Example.** The note on Vayera 18:3 brings Onkelos: first Hashem, then the three men.

**Why.** Onkelos is the oldest reading of the verse, and Ibn Ezra often agrees or argues with him. *Ruling 43.*

### 2. Rashi throughout, with the sources he rests on
**Rule.** The notes compare Rashi throughout, and name the Talmud or Midrash source when Rashi or Ibn Ezra rests on one.

**Example.** "… as in Bereshit Rabbah 19:8."

**Why.** Rashi is the reader's first point of comparison, and naming the source lets the reader look it up.

### 3. Radak where he discusses or explains Ibn Ezra
**Rule.** A short line for Radak where he discusses Ibn Ezra (by name or his view) or explains him notably. It says exactly who is quoted, never more than the text says.

**Example.** "Radak, quoting R. Moses ibn Ezra, …", or an editor's attribution in brackets, as the text has it.

**Why.** "You can add more comparisons in general also with commanders [commentators]." *Ruling 44.*

### 4. Rashbam like Rashi, wherever useful
**Rule.** A short line for Rashbam where his plain sense agrees with Ibn Ezra or reads the verse differently. His commentary on Genesis survives only in places, so where it survives is checked for each parasha. His Hebrew is quoted only where it has vowels; otherwise the note gives the sense in English.

**Example.** In Bereshit he survives only at 1:1–1:3 (lines at 1:1, 1:2 twice, and 1:3); in Noach only at 6:9, 8:15, 10:9, 10:15 and 11:4 (the notes use 6:9 and 11:4).

**Why.** He is the other great reader of the plain sense, beside Ibn Ezra. *Ruling 45.*

### 5. Ramban only where he quotes or adds to Ibn Ezra
**Rule.** A Ramban line stays when he cites Ibn Ezra's explanation, agrees with it, or builds on it. His objections to Ibn Ezra, his own different readings, and a line where he reaches a reading Ibn Ezra lists without naming him are left out. If one of those seems very relevant, ask first.

**Example.** Bereshit keeps 1:5, 2:7, 3:8 and 6:4; it removed 1:4, 1:5, 1:18, 2:3, 2:19, 2:20, 3:16, 4:7, 4:8 and 4:23. Noach 8:11 was removed.

**Why.** The notes explain Ibn Ezra; a long argument against him pulls the reader away from what he says. *Rulings 46, 47.*

### 6. Avi Ezer wherever it explains Ibn Ezra
**Rule.** The supercommentary Avi Ezer is read for every idea and every hint of Ibn Ezra, and also wherever it notably explains his plain sense or his grammar, like Radak; it is named in the note.

**Why.** "It's good when you use Avi Ezer to explain in notes." *Ruling 53.*

### 7. The preface's list of commentators stays true
**Rule.** The preface lists the commentators: "Onkelos and Rashi throughout, Rashbam where his commentary survives (in Bereshit, 1:1–3), Radak where he quotes or answers Ibn Ezra, and Ramban where he quotes or adds to Ibn Ezra's view". When these rules change, the preface changes with them.

**Why.** The preface tells the reader what to expect in every note.

## Grammar notes

### 8. A grammar note explains the point, not only states it
**Rule.** Every note on a grammar point Ibn Ezra makes says, in plain words, in this order: (1) **the plain point** — what Ibn Ezra shows about the word (root, conjugation, letter, vowel, stress, gender); (2) **"A reader might think …"** — the wrong reading the point prevents, phrased as a reader's possible misreading, never as Ibn Ezra's own words; (3) **the proof, and what it does for the word** — what it changes in the meaning, the tense, who acts, the gender, the link to another verse, or only how the verse is read aloud (said plainly when that is all). Where the "why" is Claude's inference, it says "it seems". Only the grammar sentences may be lightly reworded; **everything else in the note stays** — its Rashi, Radak, Rashbam and Ramban lines, Text lines and other comments. Where an addition is weak, it is left out. You do not have to review every grammar note; you mark Change or Remove only where you disagree.

**Example.** "A reader might think וָאֹכֵל is the participle, 'and I am eating' …"; "A reader might read וַיֵּרָא as וַיַּרְא, 'Hashem saw Abram' …".

**Why.** "At the moment I read it and just think to myself: okay, so what?" *Rulings 48, 49.*

## Ibn Ezra's ideas

### 9. Ibn Ezra's ideas get a full paragraph
**Rule.** Wherever Ibn Ezra discusses an **idea** (God's knowledge, will and names; creation; the angels; the soul; prophecy; providence; the image of God; free will), his **science** (the spheres, astronomy, astrology, the elements) or a **"secret"** ("the wise will understand", "I cannot explain", "there is a secret"), the note explains it, after the plain explanation of the comment and before the other commentators:
- **the idea**, explained in detail, with the background a reader needs (for example, the medieval picture of the spheres);
- **what a reader would otherwise have thought**;
- **why Ibn Ezra brings it here** — only where the verse itself prompts the idea.

Where he hints and stops, the note says what the hint points to and **names the source**: his own comment elsewhere, or Avi Ezer. Ramban's objections stay out (rule 5). Where the reasoning is Claude's own, it says "it seems". This applies from draft 1 of every new parasha; in the parashot already written it came as review rows. Every idea paragraph goes to you as its own review row (**08 Review**, rule 5).

**Example.** Vayera 18:21, on God's knowledge. A hint explained from his own comment elsewhere: Exodus 23:21, "He is all, and from Him is all".

**Why.** "When the commentator talks about a philosophical idea, for example Parasha Vayera 18:21 talks about the knowledge of God, make the notes focus on them and explain the concept in detail and why the commentator wants to bring this point and what you would have thought otherwise." *Ruling 52.*

### 10. Idea notes: length and sources
**Rule.**
- **A full paragraph, nothing cut,** for real ideas (God's knowledge and will, the soul, prophecy, providence, creation, the image of God, free will). When one is revised, it is added to, never cut.
- **Three or four sentences** for background (the spheres, the three worlds, the elements, astrology).
- **Rambam**, wherever Ibn Ezra's idea is one Rambam discusses (the Guide of the Perplexed, the Mishneh Torah), read on Sefaria.
- **No Aristotle.** **Never Mechokekei Yehudah** (Yahel Ohr, Karnei Ohr), which is Krinsky's.
- Every source is read on Sefaria before it is cited.

**Example.** On free will: Rambam's view is given. On the spheres: three or four sentences.

**Why.** Your answers to Audit 1: "don't cut it out", "shorten" for the background notes, "add in the whole element of free will … Rambam", "drop Aristotle". *Rulings 53, 54, 60.*

### 11. Idea notes in the old style, with Saadia and Ibn Ezra's own writings
**Rule.** Each idea keeps one full paragraph in the old style (rule 9). Inside it, where they add something:
- **Saadia Gaon** — one sentence from *Emunot veDeot* (on Sefaria as *HaEmunot veHaDeot*, Ibn Tibbon's Hebrew) wherever Saadia discusses the **same idea**, not merely the same verse, with the treatise and chapter named ("Emunot veDeot III:1") and read on Sefaria; especially where Ibn Ezra follows him, answers him or calls him "the Gaon". Where Saadia has nothing on the idea, nothing is said.
- **Ibn Ezra explained by Ibn Ezra** — a short sentence saying where he explains himself in his own writings: *Yesod Mora*, or his comment on another verse or book (Torah, Isaiah, Psalms, Job, Ecclesiastes, Daniel …), always where he says "as I will explain" or "as I explained".

**Example.** The pointer is one short sentence saying where he explains it, as you asked on the Ideas Audits: "Just say where to find the secret"; "Just say where in Deuteronomy the reader can find it." A Saadia sentence names its place: "Emunot veDeot III:1".

**Why.** "I want it in the old style", with Saadia brought in "where rav Saadia gaon says something". *Rulings 55, 56, 57.*

### 12. The idea places are found in Ibn Ezra's Hebrew too
**Rule.** Every parasha gets two searches for idea places: one by English words and a wider one by the words of Ibn Ezra's own Hebrew. Every place found is read and either gets an idea paragraph (a review row) or is recorded with the reason it has none ("not an idea: …", or "explained in the note").

**Example.** The English search missed Toledot 25:23 and 27:27; the Hebrew search found them (25:25 had nothing to add once its sources were read). It also found 15 places in Bereshit to Chayei Sarah, all already explained in their notes; you chose Noach 9:4 for a paragraph.

**Why.** "In section F why are there only 5 items, can there be more?" *Ruling 58.*

### 13. Every idea sentence is checked against its source
**Rule.** Every sentence of an idea paragraph that rests on a source is checked against that source as read on Sefaria: supported, partly supported, or not supported. A sentence that says more than its source is rewritten before you see it. One that stays "partly" needs an accepted reason: Claude's own reasoning marked "it seems", the "what a reader would think" clause, or background you asked for.

**Example.** The first check corrected Toledot F1, F5, F10, F12, F14, F16 and F17, and found that Bereshit Ideas Audit B3 and B9, already approved, said more than Saadia does; they were put to you before they were applied.

**Why.** "Do all of them" — the list of improvements. *Ruling 59.*

## What a note never says

### 14. A note is part of the book
**Rule.** A note never mentions the review list ("this is a row in the review list") and never says that something "has not been checked". Either it is checked, or it is left out and asked about in the review.

**Why.** The reader holds a finished book, not a working draft.

### 15. No remarks about Sefaria or the printed headings
**Rule.** A note never says that a quotation "agrees with Sefaria's text", "(checked on Sefaria)", "Sefaria's text" or "Sefaria's comment has no headword", and makes no remark about the printed headword or the heading of a comment. Where a text is meant, it says "the Masoretic text". A note says something about the text only when the quotation differs from the Masoretic text or the print has a misprint — in a short *Text.* line. If nothing differs, there is no Text line. Sefaria is named only in the credits in the front matter (its licence requires it). The "Uncertain" labels are covered in **04 Translation**, rule 4.

**Why.** Remarks about how the book was checked are not part of the commentary. *Ruling 50.*

### 16. No source line at the end of each section
**Rule.** The English commentary does not end each section with a source line. The sources are credited once, in the preface (the Masorah text's licence requires the credit there).

**Example.** Removed: "Sefaria: Genesis 1:1-5 (Miqra according to the Masorah); Ibn Ezra on Genesis 1:1-5 (Piotrkow, 1907–1911)".

**Why.** Once in the preface is enough; on every section it clutters the page. *Ruling 51.*

### 17. A verse quoted in a note: the Hebrew, then the English
**Rule.** When a note quotes a verse (or a few words of one), it gives the Hebrew words, pointed, then their English in quotation marks, then the reference.

**Example.** Not “Take, I pray, your son” (22:2) alone, but the Hebrew of those words from 22:2 followed by “Take, I pray, your son” (22:2).

**Why.** "Remember to make the notes have also the Hebrew when quoting as well as the translations." *Ruling 110.*
